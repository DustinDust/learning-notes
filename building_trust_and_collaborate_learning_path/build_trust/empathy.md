# 1. Convey your warmth and concern

This one about a manager who's super competent but team dont fully trust him, people hold back idea and never give critical feedback. Author explain: competency answer "can you do it" but empathy answer "do you actually care about me". Research says empathetic leader = more innovative team, better retention. Author then gives bunch of physical/verbal tip to "warm up":

- tilt head slightly, keep posture open not rigid
- smile genuine (eyes crease, cheekbone lift - not just mouth corner, that's fake smile)
- speak softer, not booming (booming = power, soft = compassion)
- ask non-work question, show interest in their life (example given: asking about someone kid's first day of school)
- use tentative language when want people to disagree comfortably, eg "I'm not sure but maybe we should.." (note: opposite of what you do if you want to project power/expertise)
- say "we/our" instead of "I/me"
- for remote colleague: leave voicemail instead of email (tone of voice come through), turn camera ON so people see smile/nod
- use people name in meeting, credit other, over-appreciate (can't overdo this one apparently)

=> End exercise: think of 1 person who might doubt your good intent toward them, write 3 small action to show you care.

## Personal Notes

- competency cue vs empathy cue = 2 different question people ask about you, both need to be answer
- genuine smile detail is interesting, (eyes not just mouth) - good to remember for video call
- action step at end (name 1 person + 3 action) is good format, might reuse for my own team

# 2. Listen and empathize

Listening is describe as boosting all 3 trust driver (empathy, competency, authenticity) at once. Key point: doesnt matter if your actually listening, if it dont LOOK like your listening, trust dont build. Tips:

- nod, eye contact, arrange webcam so you can see faces while eye stay near camera
- tell people "im taking note" out loud, or show notepad, otherwise looks like you not paying attention
- never interrupt - and this include side-chatting in chat box during meeting while someone talk (mention this explicitly as rude)
- signal wanting to speak by leaning in + gesture, or raise-hand emoji in video call
- remove distraction if you know you tend to lose focus: clear desk, close tab, phone far away
- keep camera ON (help you focus + shows engagement); if seeing your own face distract you, use hide-self-view or literally put post it note over your own video
- when responding: summarize what other person said IN YOUR OWN WORD first, then validate their emotion if they express one. this make them feel heard and also help YOU stay focused since you know you gotta summarize after

# 3. Be intentional about touching base

Staying in contact = show you care = build trust. In office, easy: chit chat before meeting start, walk around office. Remote work need more intentional effort:

- make chart: teammate name, location, timezone, last time you talk to them
  - or just randomly point at org chart and reach out to whoever
- between "real" conversation, send meme/quote/thinking-of-you message
- mention of "Donut" app - randomly pair 2 people in org to connect every few week, friend used it 11 month straight and met people company-wide. I won't use that app though, sounds like a hassle to connect randomly
- schedule 20 min coffee chat / virtual lunch, no agenda, just catch up
- before call end, schedule NEXT one already
- for onsite worker: be "feet on the ground"; for remote colleague, pop in and ask quick question on their behalf so they dont feel isolated

====> closing line: "real estate motto is location location location, trust motto is communicate communicate communicate"

4. Assume positive intent

Story: new teacher ask mentor what to do when student sleep in class, mentor just say "talk softer near them, put jacket on them" (handsomely) instead of punishing. Turn out mentor's class have best engagement because student trust him, and he trust them (assume they were up late working, not being lazy).
=> Core idea: human tend to blame PERSONALITY ("they lazy/rude") over SITUATION ("maybe they had bad night"), and this hurt trust.

3 tips to assume positive intent:

- Notice when your blaming someone's personality, pause when you feel annoyed (author literally cover her mouth sometime to stop herself talking)
- Rewrite the story: think "no one trying to be mean" and invent situational reason instead. example given: colleague interrupt you in meeting - instead of "she trying to steal my idea" think "maybe bad audio connection" or "she excited and trying to support me"
- Ask and listen, get full story, actively try to DISPROVE your own negative assumption

!! Also mention Brene Brown's technique: literally say to the person "the story I'm telling myself is ..." out loud, this open door for them to clarify without accusation.

Last part: what if person really IS a jerk? still act with positive intent anyway.

## Personal Notes

- personality-blame vs situational-attribution is literally a psychology concept (fundamental attribution error) even tho she dont name it directly
- "story I'm telling myself is..." phrase is really practical, feels less confrontational than direct accusation
  => this is probably hardest driver for me personally, my first reaction usually go straight to personality-blame not situational
