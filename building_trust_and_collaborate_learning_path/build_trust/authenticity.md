# 1. Make sure your actions match your words

Summary

Story of Dana, exhausted from zoom fatigue (4th meeting that day), she want to support colleague idea but on camera she look flat/disengaged, barely talk. Colleague confront her after, she insist "I am supportive" but he dont believe her, cause her non-verbal didnt match her word. This is about congruity between what you SAY and how you actually LOOK/ACT.

Tips:

- protect your calendar, be more selective what meeting you join, so you have energy to actually show up right
- glance at your own self-view window during call, check your own body language (you might not realize how you look)
- use real background on video call (not virtual bg), it's small signal of authenticity, seeing kid or pet walk by humanize you
- use speaker/headset on audio call so hand are free, this help you sound more natural. (example: nurse doing telehealth call use speakerphone so she can talk with hand like normal, patient cant see her but it change HER tone/energy)
- audit your calendar + sent email: does it match what you SAY you value? eg if you say "learning is important to me" but calendar has zero time block for reading/class, that's incongruent
  **Notes**
- congruity = alignment between internal state/value and external behavior, basically "walk the talk" but at micro level (body language, tone) not just big promise
- self-view check is simple but i never think to do this mid-meeting, only check how i look before call start
- calendar-audit idea is good, kind of uncomfortable gonna be honest, i probably fail this if i check my own calendar vs what i claim matter to me

# 2. Self disclose to build trust

Summary

Story: colleague who never talk about family, one day accidentally mention "need to leave early for my kids" - this open door, others start sharing too, team become closer and performance actually improve. Point: self-disclosure (share about yourself/feeling) = signal of authenticity = build trust.

Different form of self disclosure:

- share non-work stories (weekend anecdote etc), see how people respond
- share FAILURE not just win, normalize setback (leader admitting bad decision give other permission to also take risk/fail)
- share feeling about current event, not just fact (example: author telling student she was "terrified" when class went remote, students felt less alone after hearing that)
- take a stand, speak mind honestly but tactfully. she give example of colleague asking "can you support my idea" and she didnt like it - options was: lie ("sure sounds great"), half-truth (technically avoid answering), be brutal ("your idea is terrible"), be vague. She instead said plain honest thing: "Jose idea seems better and here's why" - kind but direct.
  Important caveat: she acknowledge self-disclosure have DIFFERENT COST depending on who you are (some people face bigger risk being open, due to bias etc), so she say "weigh cost/benefit for yourself" - not one-size-fit-all advice. For those with less cost, she ask to actively support teammate who do risk being vulnerable, and to normalize other people's uniqueness too.

# 3. Avoid bias while detecting trustworthiness

Summary

This one flip perspective: not how to appear trustworthy, but how to accurately JUDGE trust in other, without falling into bias. Opening joke: wish there a buzzer for lying, or Pinocchio nose, but reality we bad at detecting lie from body language alone (research back this up).

Possible (unreliable) deception cue:

- closed posture (crossed arm, leaning away) - but might just be cold
- fidgeting with face/throat - could be nervousness not lying
- too much/little eye contact - but this is culturally different, not universal signal
- vague explanation - could just be someone who's concise, not hiding something
  None of these alone mean much, only if MULTIPLE cue stack up together, worth asking more question.

Bigger warning: social psychology show dominant demographic group in a culture get "automatic credibility" more easily, and we naturally trust people who resemble ourselves more. This is a bias, not real signal of trustworthiness.

3 step to counter this bias:

1. get SPECIFIC about your doubt - "bad feeling" isnt a real reason, ask what exactly bother you (missed deadline? avoided eye contact?)
2. challenge the negative assumption - list out other explanation (avoid eye contact could be shy, cultural, or just allergy)
3. actively seek CONTRADICTING evidence - notice when person do show up on time, notice when other speak well of them, dont just confirm your existing bias
   Ending line: no magic buzzer exist, but you can detect trust reasonably while staying aware of your own bias.

## Personal Notes

- this is the most "meta" video in whole section, its not about being trustworthy but judging OTHER accurately, good balance to have in the course
- "we trust people who look/sound/act like us" is uncomfortable truth, this basically affinity bias, relevant especially in hiring or team-forming decision
- 3-step debias process (specify -> challenge -> seek contradiction) feel reusable outside trust topic too, basically generic bias-correction framework
