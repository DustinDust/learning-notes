## 1. Getting distracted by internal noise

Our brain use mental filters to sort all the information around us. This is useful because it help us focus on what matter, but dirty filters can make us hear what we expect instead of what speaker actually say.

Filters can come from:

1. past experience
2. education and professional expertise
3. strong belief or assumptions
4. emotional hot buttons

The doctors example show this clearly. Each doctor explained the foot pain using their own specialty, while the actual cause was simply the ambulance door hitting the foot.

Some warning signs:

- finishing someone sentence in my head
- preparing a counter argument while they still speak
- reacting quickly to certain words or topics
- assuming I already know what they mean

To clean the filter, set aside assumptions for a moment and listen to understand first.

**Notes**

- Very relevant for engineering discussions. Sometimes I already think I know the root cause before other person finish explaining.
- Need to seperate understanding from agreeing. I can understand first and disagree later.

### 2. Multitasking

Multitasking is a big barrier to listening. Switching between a conversation, phone, messages, and other work make us slower and cause more errors. It also make the other person feel they are not important.

A great listener makes the speaker feel like they are the only other person in the room.

Two better options:

1. Stop other tasks and give full attention.
2. If now is a bad time, say it honestly and arrange another time when I can listen properly.

Pretending to listen while working does not really save time. The person may need to repeat everything later, and some trust is lost too.

**Notes**

- I do this quite often with Slack or running some claude code while in a meeting.
- Saying "can we talk after lunch" is better than giving half attention (this is especially bad when working-from-home).

### 3. Paying attention to delivery

Bad delivery can distract us from useful content. Examples are too many filler words, reading from slides, speaking too softly, mumbling, or having an accent that is hard for us to understand.

If the delivery is only annoying:

- notice it once, tell myself "noted", then go back to content
- mentally paraphrase what speaker is saying
- ask what summary I would give to another person

If the delivery prevents understanding, dont fake it, ask the person to repeat, speak louder, or slow down a bit. Pretending can lead to a much worse and awkward misunderstanding later.

**Notes**

- The story about hearing "the kids came over" instead of "the cat got ran over" is funny but makes the point very well.
- Asking for clarification is not rude. Fake understanding is probaly more rude in long run.

### 4. Overwhelming yourself with information

Trying to write every word makes us fall behind and understand less. Notes should be selective based on how the information will be used.

If I am learning, capture:

- big picture
- general principles
- main development or idea

If I need to act later, capture:

- exact deadline
- important detail
- action item, maybe in a seperate `To Do` section

Writing notes by hand can be more effective than typing, but the important thing is finding a note taking method that works and using it consistently.

Never use a sentence when a phrase is enough. Never use a phrase when one word is enough.

**Notes**

- This is also why meeting transcript is not same as useful meeting notes.
- I should decide first if I need concept, detail, or action. Then note only that.

### 5. Responding inappropriately

Listening does not end when speaker stops. The first response tells them if we really listened or was only waiting for our turn.

Four common response mistakes:

1. Making it about myself: replying with my own similar story.
2. Criticizing immediately: even subtle judgement can shut the person down.
3. Giving advice without being asked: helpful intention does not always feel helpful.
4. Focusing only on facts: knowing small details but missing the feeling or main point.

A better response is usually simple. Acknowledge the emotion and overall meaning first.

Before responding, think what the speaker need right now (!important). Maybe they want advice, but maybe they only want to share, be understood, or feel heard.

**Notes**

- I am probably most guilty of giving solution too early. Typical engineer response haha.
- Need to ask before advice: "do you want idea, or just want me to listen?"
- Good listening is not only receiving info. Response is also part of it.
