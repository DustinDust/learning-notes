### 1. View conflict in a new way

Conflict is part of life, but how we see it affects how we handle it. Some people avoid it, some deny having any, and some think they're good at it while coming across as harsh. We often see conflict as:

- someone must win and someone must lose
- something personal, "they wronged me"
- an ongoing power struggle, always defending or attacking

Even the usual definition sounds "exhausting": a serious disagreement or argument, often lasting a long time. No wonder people dont want to get involved lol.

Author suggest another way to look at it: opportunity to grow, understand each other better, and get back to the shared mission. Seeing it this way make it easier to address things sooner.

Example: executive felt his marketing manager was undermining him by going directly to his boss for requests. She was also becoming friends with his boss, so he took it personally. His own productivity suffered and he planned to fire her, didnt see another option.

After coaching, he saw a chance to clarify roles and responsibilities instead. He had TWO conversations, one with his boss and one with the marketing manager, to bring everyone back to the organization's mission.

**Personal Notes**

- (connect back to "assume positive intent" in Building Trust). Easy to make a story about someone trying to undermine me before checking whats actually happening
- useful question: what needs to become clearer here? Gives the conversation a purpose beyond proving who's right
- author say if there isnt any conflict, maybe there isnt much growth either (type shit). I think this is very true because conflict is the catalyst for growth.

### 2. Understand what makes a conversation difficult

Same conversation can feel straightforward to one person and really uncomfortable to another. Author share a colleague saying "that doesn't sound that difficult" about her experience, then explaining how she would handle it. But she didnt understand what made it hard for the author.

4 variables to unpack here:

1. Situation: something is happening that I dont want, or something I want isnt happening
2. Relationship: how much do I care about this person? Am I worried about damaging the relationship?
3. Power structure: are they my superior, equal, or someone reporting to me?
4. Emotions: what feeling come up for me, and what might the other person feel or express?

Author's example because I dont work in sales lol: Kim keeps calling after meeting at a business lunch. Coffee together turned out to be a chance for Kim to sell expensive vitamins and health products. Need to say I'm not interested and dont like the pressure.

- situation: unwanted sales calls
- relationship: like Kim but dont have much history, still need to see each other on a committee
- power: equal, Kim cant fire me
- emotions: worry about hurting her feelings, getting cold shoulder later, making future meetings awkward

Just imagining her reaction creates tension, so avoiding it starts to feel easier... then Kim calls again.

=> Exercise: pick a difficult conversation and write down these 4 parts. For relationship, rate how much you care from 1 to 10 and describe the perceived risk. This gives more clarity before starting the actual conversation.

**Personal Notes**

- "what exactly makes this hard?" is more useful than just calling the whole thing difficult
- equal power doesnt automatically mean easy conversation. In Kim's example most of the discomfort come from imagined reaction and having to meet again
- connects to listening course: saying "thats not difficult" skips understanding the other person's experience

### 3. Identify the power structure and patterns in conflict

Power affects how safe we feel speaking up. Author explain that talking to someone with higher status can put us in protective mode, since our brain tries to avoid threats.

3 levels to identify from my own position: do I have LESS, EQUAL, or MORE power than the other person?

Then look at the patterns between us. Some common ones:

- avoidance
- shutting down
- aggression
- sarcasm
- crying

=> Sometimes changing our own response makes room for a different response from the other person. Cant directly change their pattern or erase the past, but can work on our own part.

Before the conversation, ask:

1. How much power do I have relative to them?
2. What do they usually do?
3. What do I usually do in response?
4. What could I do differently this time?

**Personal Notes**

- easier to notice "they always do this" than "and I always respond like that". Second part is probably where the useful work starts
- silence connects directly to effective listening. Small pause can give the conversation space, even if it doesnt feel comfortable
- changing my response might help, but doesnt guarantee the other person will change. Author's example shows a possibility
