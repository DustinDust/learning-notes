### 1. Learn what to do and not to do in a difficult conversation

Author give 4 main dos and donts:

a. Control emotions and dont start the conversation when overly emotional

-> Emotions are useful signal that a conversation need to happen, but timing still matter. Calm down first and think about the end result you actually want.

b. Talk face to face, dont handle the conflict through digital communication

-> Writing feels easier when upset because we can hide behind email, text, or chat. Instead of sending it, write privately or journal about what happened. Digital message is especially bad for performance problems, arguments, pointing out flaws, and defending yourself because tone is easy to misread.

If an email triggers you:

- dont reply immediately
- dont forward it to other people
- dont collect opinions to prove your side
- ask for face to face conversation, or a phone call if thats not possible

c. Seek understanding and avoid finger pointing

-> Anger makes it easy to blame, usually based on assumptions about the other person's intent. This will make them defensive and doesnt create collaboration. Hear their side before deciding they are the enemy. You cant really bully someone into collaborating with you.

d. Stay focused and dont get distracted

->Difficult conversation can easily turn into argument about old or unrelated problem. Keep the desired end result in mind so you can return to the actual issue when discussion goes off track.

=> Good judgment can stop a charged situation from becoming chaos and help turn it into collaboration.

**Personal Notes**

- journaling can be good middle step, get all the angry version out without making another person deal with it
- "dont take the bait" is helpful for emails. A message being sent doesnt mean it requires immediate answer
- keeping end result in mind connect back to viewing conflict as way to get back to the mission

### 2. Learn best practices to prepare for a difficult conversation

3 best practices for preparing:

a. Manage the narrative before it manages you

We tell ourself stories about why other people act a certain way, then slowly treat those stories like proven fact. Negative or repeated thoughts can shape the conversation before it even starts.

Ask whats my current narrative about this person? Which parts are things I actually know, and which part did I assume?

b. Cultivate curiosity

Acting like a know it all closes possible explanations. If I think I already know someone's motive and response, there is no reason left to ask them anything.

This relate to confirmation bias: tendency to notice and interpret evidence that supports what we already believe. Basically, we get what we look for.

c. Go straight to the source

Other people confirming my experience doesnt replace talking to the person involved.

Questions to prepare:

1. What narrative have I created about the other person?
2. What questions could challenge my bias and start real dialogue?
3. Have I spoken directly with the source of the problem?

=> Main formula: manage narrative, stay curious, go to source.
