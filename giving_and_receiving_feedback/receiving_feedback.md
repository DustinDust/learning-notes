# Create a growth mindset with feedback

## Main idea

Fixed mindset vs growth mindset:

- Fixed mindset: you're born with a certain amount of ability, and that's basically your ceiling
- Growth mindset: ability, talent and strength can all be developed, potential is unlimited
  - Growth mindset goal: just be a bit better today than you were yesterday

## Why growth mindset matters for feedback

- It lets you see new situations and challenges as a learning curve, not a threat
- It puts you in control of how you interpret feedback, instead of feedback controlling your emotions
- You focus on what you can still achieve, not what you failed to achieve before
- If you accept in advance that you'll make mistakes and get feedback you don't like, the whole process feels less painful
  - Example: learning to ride a bike, falling off hurts, but it's part of how you actually learn

## Two types of feedback

- Evaluative feedback: factual, like exam grades or pass/fail results. Useful but not much detail
- Developmental feedback: opinions and ideas from others on how you can improve, tweak your approach, or get better at something specific. This is the type that really helps build a growth mindset

# Use feedback to write your story

## Main idea

Same event can feel totally different depending on your mood. Your thoughts and feelings basically "write the story" for you, and this same thing happens with feedback too.

## How mood affects how you take feedback

- If you're angry, tired or overwhelmed, even well-meant advice can feel like the final push that makes you feel like a failure
- You might start thinking "I never do anything right" or "I'll never improve", which is not really true, just your mood talking
- If you're happy and confident, you're more likely to actually listen and use the feedback without getting too stuck on it
  => The trick: use feedback constructively without fixating on it, no matter what mood you're in (though it's harder when you're in bad mood)

Remember:

- Feedback is just one person's opinion, even if a few people say same thing
- Everyone has development areas, everyone makes mistake, this is normal
- What matters is what you actually do with the feedback afterward

## 4 steps to take control of your reaction

- Take a step back, don't react right away. If you feel low, it's fine to tell the person "I'll take this on board but need time to process it"
- Don't fixate on negative feedback (or positive either). Being overly positive about yourself isn't realistic and can create blind spot. Try to look at feedback from balanced angle
- Separate opinion from fact. An opinion is just one person's view, someone else might see it totally different, so focus on facts and stay open to different opinions
- Create forward focus. It's good to know what you can improve, but don't dwell on the past

## Exercise: Write Your Story

1. Think of a recent time you got feedback
2. Write the story of that event with a fully negative view
3. Write the same story again but with a fully positive view
4. Compare both versions
5. Choose your own story, using both perspectives, and use it to grow

# The grace game

## Main idea

> Feedback from others is a really valuable tool to help you improve and grow. You need to appreciate it even when you don't agree with it, because someone actually took time and effort to give you an observation or advice, either to help you improve or to tell you what you're doing well.

## Why asking for feedback matters

- You won't always know how you come across to others, or the best way to tackle something
- Only way to really improve is to understand your development areas and your strengths
- Asking for feedback also helps build stronger relationships with people

## 5 tips on how to ask for and receive feedback

1. Ask for feedback on something specific, not vague. If you ask vague question, you get vague answer. Example: instead of "how am I doing", ask "how did I handle the stakeholder meeting on this project"
2. Always be gracious and grateful when you receive feedback, whether you agree with it or not. You don't have to use every piece of feedback, that depends how useful it is, but you can still thank the person for taking the time
3. Try to get a rounded perspective, don't just ask one person. If a few people mention the same thing, that's probably a real development area worth paying attention to
4. Pay attention to indirect feedback too, not just what people say directly. Watch body language and behavior, like someone avoiding you or being slow to respond. Try to figure out why
5. Take a step back and stay objective. If feedback makes you feel like your performance isn't good enough, try to reframe it. Even top athletes and successful people still have development areas and still look for feedback to keep improving
