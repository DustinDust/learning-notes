# The Potential Trap

## Main idea

Feedback helps you improve. Athletes always get feedback and use it to get better. But in normal life (work, personal), people often avoid feedback. This is a mistake.

## Why feedback feels hard

- Getting feedback can feel like a personal attack, not just about your work
- Giving feedback can feel risky — you worry you might upset the person

## How to give good feedback

- Talk about how the person can improve, not just what is wrong
- Be specific. Don't say "you always do X" instead say exactly what action to change
- Specific feedback is easier to act on

## How to ask for feedback

- Ask "What can I do better?" instead of "What am I doing wrong?"
  => This makes the answer more positive and helpful

## The "potential trap"

- If you never ask for feedback, you stay stuck
- You won't know your weak points, so you can't improve
- Like an athlete who needs a coach to notice a wrong movement you would need someone outside to see what you can't see

## Questions to think about

=> When you get feedback, does it feel good or bad?
=> Do you feel thankful or uncomfortable?
=> What can you do to avoid getting stuck (the "potential trap")?

# Unveil your blind spots with feedback

## Main idea

Blind spots are things which you cannot see, but other people can. Feedback is one of best way to expose them.

## What is blind spots?

- Occasion when your actions or words dont come across like what you mean;
  - Example: you focus hard at work, but other people see you as an "antisocial" person.
  - Example: you want to make good relationship, but this make you scared to push back when it is needed.

## Why we got blind spots

- We react to thing automatic, based on old habits and experience.
  => This is fast, but also can make us "lazy": we always stuck in same pattern.
- To see new options, we'll need other people perspective (which is feedback);

Exercise for finding blind spots:

- Think about recent situation that was hard for you.
- Write down: What was you thinking? What did you do? What was the result?
  - Example: Customer complaint arrived. You wanted to fix it fast, so you told others what must be done. It was fixed, but people don't want to provide you with assistance in the future.
- Write down all the ways others maybe see your behaviors, both good and bad.
  - Example: Maybe they think you are efficient and just really care about customers, or maybe they think you are a pushy person and don't listen to their opinions.
- Write down any feedbacks you got, words, writing, or body language (sighing, roll eyes, smile, nod head).
  - Pay attention to body language, this usually shows real feeling (important!).
  - Now look all the things that you wrote. What does this tell you about your blind spots?

Final step:

=> Ask other people if your blind spot is correct or not; (E.g I've noticed I've been ABC, do you think the same?)
=> Next time the same situation happens, remember this blind spot and try to act different way.

# The psychology of perspective

## Main idea

When we get negative feedback, our first reaction is usually "no, this can't be right, they don't really understand." But sometimes later, once we calm down, we realize there was actually some truth in it. This is mostly because of our ego.

## Why we reject feedback at first

- The situation is heated and emotions are high, so it's hard to think clearly.
- Ego's job is to protect us, to keep us from feeling pain or discomfort;
- When feedback goes against how we see ourselves, ego steps in and distorts it, making us think it's wrong even when it's not
- This protects us from feeling guilty, ashamed or anxious

**But ego can also work the opposite way:**

- If we've been in a negative mindset lately, ego gets a bit lazy and just assumes everything is bad, including the feedback.
  => We end up confirming what we already believe about ourselves, even if the feedback was actually useful!

## Key point

- Our perspective isn't a fact, its just one opinion.
- Feedback also isn't 100% accurate, its just someone else's opinion
- We need to manage our ego so we can stay more objective about things

## Exercise: Reporter Perspective

- Think of a recent situation that was challenging, and describe it from your own point of view: what happened, who was involved, what you were thinking, how you reacted, what was the outcome?
- Now do the same thing again, but pretend you're an objective reporter who knows nothing about the background.
  - Describe what happened, who was involved, how everyone reacted, what the outcome was for all people involved (not just you)
  - Compare both versions. Where's the difference between your view and the "reporter" view?
  - Example: you thought you looked indecisive because you asked for many opinions before deciding, but actually you came across as collaborative

    => Ask yourself: was my perspective really accurate? Did ego distort the picture somehow?

## Final point

- Everyone sees things a bit differently, perspective is just an opinion.
- Gathering different perspectives gives a clearer picture of what's really going on
- We can't rely only on our own view because ego sometimes distorts it, that's why we need feedback from others.
